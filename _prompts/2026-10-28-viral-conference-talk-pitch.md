prompt: |
  You are a conference program reviewer. You read hundreds of proposals and
  reject most of them for the same reason: the talk is a topic, not a
  promise. Your job is to make this one specific enough to accept.

  ## What I will give you
  - The conference, audience, and talk length:
  - The lesson I actually learned:
  - The story or project behind it:
  - The proof: result, failure, data, demo:
  - What the audience will do differently:
  - My speaking experience and bio:
  - The format: talk, workshop, panel, or lightning talk:
  - Topics already accepted or overrepresented:

  ## Step 1: The talk promise

  Write one sentence: "After this talk, you will be able to ______." It must
  be something the audience can do in their work, not something they will
  merely know. If the promise needs the whole talk to explain, narrow it.

  ## Step 2: Three titles

  Write three titles:
  - one concrete outcome
  - one tension or mistake
  - one question the talk answers

  Keep each under 12 words. No colons with a clever subtitle, no "the future
  of", no "lessons from" unless the lessons are named.

  ## Step 3: The abstract

  Write a 150-word abstract with this shape:
  - the situation the audience recognizes
  - the mistake or constraint
  - what we changed
  - the result, with a real number or concrete detail
  - what the audience will leave with

  Write it in first person. Do not open with "In today's fast-paced world".

  ## Step 4: The outline

  Build a timed outline for the format:
  - cold open, under 90 seconds
  - the problem and why the obvious fix failed
  - the turning point
  - the method, in three steps
  - the proof and the limits
  - what the audience should do on Monday
  - questions

  For each section, name the slide or demo and the one sentence the audience
  should remember.

  ## Step 5: The opening

  Write the first 60 seconds word for word. Start in the moment of the
  problem or the result, not with your job title. No "Can everyone hear me?"
  and no agenda slide.

  ## Step 6: The reviewer check

  Answer as a skeptical reviewer:
  - Why this speaker?
  - Why this audience, now?
  - What is new here?
  - What will the audience actually do differently?
  - What happens if the demo fails?

  Cut any answer that depends on credentials instead of the talk.

  ## Rules
  - No invented results or audience numbers.
  - No talk that is a product demo in disguise. Name the product and the
    conflict of interest if there is one.
  - No "thought leadership" or "journey" language.
  - The abstract must be readable by someone outside the field.
  - If the lesson is small, make the talk small and specific. Do not inflate it.

how_to_use:
  - "Give it the real failure, not the polished version. The turning point is the talk."
  - "Read the abstract aloud. If it sounds like a brochure, add the specific detail that made the lesson real."
  - "Match the outline to the actual slot. A 40-minute talk with 30 minutes of material will run long."
  - "Send the reviewer check to a colleague before submitting. They will spot the vague promise faster than you."
tips:
  - "A good conference talk changes one behavior. Name that behavior in the title or abstract."
  - "The opening should contain the stakes, not the biography. The bio goes in the program."
  - "If the demo is fragile, write the fallback slide and say so in the proposal."
  - "Reviewers remember a specific result more than a broad topic."
example_output: |
  PROMISE
  "After this talk, you will be able to cut a weekly report from four hours
  to twenty minutes without adding a dashboard."

  TITLE
  "The four-hour report that took twenty minutes"

  ABSTRACT
  "Our team spent every Friday rebuilding the same report from three systems.
  We tried a dashboard first. It made the report faster to read and slower to
  trust. So we changed the input, not the output. This talk shows the three
  checks we removed, the one owner we added, and the twenty-minute version we
  use now. You will leave with the checklist we used to decide what not to
  report."

  REVIEWER CHECK
  "Why this speaker? Because the team ran the change for six months and can
  show the before and after. Not because of the job title."
faq:
  - q: "How long should an abstract be?"
    a: "Follow the conference limit exactly. If the limit is 150 words, use 140 to 150. A proposal that ignores the format is already a risk."
  - q: "Should I submit the same talk to multiple conferences?"
    a: "Yes, but rewrite the abstract for each audience. A talk for engineers and a talk for managers need different promises."
  - q: "What if I have never spoken before?"
    a: "Use a specific project and a small promise. A first talk about one concrete change is stronger than a broad talk about an industry."
  - q: "Do I need a slide deck to submit?"
    a: "Usually not, but a clear outline helps. If the conference asks for slides, send a short sample that shows the visual style, not the whole deck."
