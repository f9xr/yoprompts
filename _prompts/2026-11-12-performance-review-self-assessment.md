excerpt: "Turns a year of half-remembered work into a self-assessment that states what you did, what changed, and what you need, without bragging or apologising."
prompt: |
  You are a manager who has written and read hundreds of performance reviews,
  and who has watched good work get overlooked because the write-up was vague.
  You help people state their case plainly. You do not inflate and you do not
  coach anyone into sounding like a brochure.

  ## What I will give you
  - My role, level, and the review period:
  - What was I actually measured on (goals, OKRs, targets, or "nothing formal"):
  - What I did, in rough notes, including the things that went badly:
  - Numbers I have, and how confident I am in them:
  - What my manager already knows about:
  - What I want from the review: a rating, a raise, a change of scope, or
    just no surprises:

  ## Produce in this order

  1. **Period summary.** Three to four sentences: the job I was hired to do,
     the job I ended up doing, and the one sentence I want my manager to
     repeat to their manager.

  2. **Evidence table.** Every piece of work as: what I did, what changed,
     how I know, and who else can confirm it. If a claim has no evidence,
     mark it as a claim, not a result.

  3. **Outcomes versus activity.** Sort the list into things that changed a
     number or a decision, and things that were work but produced no change.
     Be honest about the size of each pile.

  4. **The self-assessment itself.** Written in my voice, first person,
     in the sections my company uses (results, collaboration, growth, or
     whatever the form asks). Short paragraphs, no stacked adjectives.

  5. **The miss section.** One or two real misses, each with what I learned
     and what I changed. A self-assessment with no misses reads as either
     dishonest or incurious.

  6. **What I need.** Two or three specific asks: a scope change, a skill,
     a resource, a stretch assignment. Phrased as a request, not a complaint.

  7. **Questions to ask my manager.** Four questions that surface how I am
     actually being read, especially about the gap between my view and
     theirs.

  ## Hard rules
  - Never invent a metric. If a number would help and I did not give one,
    leave a visible placeholder and ask me for it.
  - Do not use "I was responsible for". Say what I did and what changed.
  - No adjectives that assert instead of showing: passionate, dedicated,
    instrumental, key, critical.
  - Do not hide the misses. Do not dwell on them either. One paragraph each.
  - Match the company's form and vocabulary. If I tell you the review has
    four sections, use those four and no others.
  - Keep the whole thing under the length my manager will read. If it is
    over a page and a half, cut the weakest item, not the evidence.
workflow:
  - "Summarize the period in a few sentences"
  - "Build the evidence table and mark unsupported claims"
  - "Separate outcomes from activity"
  - "Draft the self-assessment in the company's sections"
  - "Write the miss section and the asks"
  - "Give four questions for the review conversation"
output_preview: "A short period summary, an evidence table, outcomes split from activity, a first-person draft in your review form's sections, honest misses, two or three specific asks, and questions for the conversation."
how_to_use:
  - "Dump the messy notes first, in any order. Sorting is the prompt's job, and the mess is where the evidence lives."
  - "Say what you were measured on. A self-assessment scored against formal goals is a different document from one scored against nothing."
  - "Name the outcome you want before you start. Ratings, raises and scope changes all need a different emphasis."
  - "Paste the review form's section names. The draft will match the shape your manager is already filling in."
tips:
  - "Write the review you want your manager to give you. Most managers are assembling a case from memory and your document can be the best evidence in it."
  - "One outcome per line, with the change named. A line that could describe any competent person in the role is filler."
  - "Put the strongest result first. Reviewers read the opening and skim the rest, so the rest has to survive a skim."
  - "Save this between cycles. Six months of notes written at the time beats one afternoon of reconstruction."
example_output: |
  PERIOD SUMMARY
  Hired to own the onboarding funnel for a self-serve product. Ended up
  owning activation end to end after the growth team was reorganized. The
  sentence I want repeated: onboarding went from the top complaint in sales
  calls to a non-issue.

  EVIDENCE TABLE
  - Rebuilt the onboarding checklist. Activation in week one rose from 34% to
    51% over two quarters. Evidence: the product dashboard, charted monthly.
    Confirmed by the analytics lead.
  - Cut time-to-first-value from nine days to two. Evidence: cohort report.
    Confirmed by support volume dropping 40% on setup tickets.
  - Ran the migration of 1,200 legacy accounts. No downtime. Evidence: the
    migration log and zero P1s that month.
  - Started a weekly onboarding review. Claim, not a result: it changed how
    we prioritised, but I have no number for it.

  OUTCOMES VERSUS ACTIVITY
  Outcomes: the three items above with a number attached. Activity: the
  redesign of the empty states, which shipped but nobody measured, and the
  two abandoned experiments.

  DRAFT (results section)
  I rebuilt the onboarding path around the first value moment rather than
  the first feature tour. Week-one activation moved from 34% to 51%, and
  setup tickets fell by 40% because the steps that generated them were
  removed rather than explained.

  MISSES
  I ran the pricing experiment without agreeing the success metric first, so
  when it came back flat we could not tell whether the idea failed or the
  measurement did. I now write the metric and the threshold before the build
  starts, every time.

  WHAT I NEED
  1. Ownership of activation formally, not just in practice.
  2. A design partner for the next quarter so I am not doing both the
     research and the build.

  QUESTIONS FOR MY MANAGER
  1. Where does my view of this year differ from yours?
  2. What would have to be true for me to be considered for the next level?
  3. Which of my results is least visible to you, and why?
  4. What should I stop doing next quarter?
faq:
  - q: "Is this dishonest if I did not hit my goals?"
    a: "No, as long as the misses are named. A review that admits a miss and shows what changed reads better than one that quietly reframes the goal, and managers usually already know the number."
  - q: "My company has no rating or formal process. Is this still useful?"
    a: "Yes. Keep the evidence table and the asks, drop the form sections, and use it as the basis of a conversation about scope and pay. The document is the point, not the form."
  - q: "Should I write about a colleague's contribution?"
    a: "Only to credit them, never to compare. Write about your work, and say plainly who else was involved, because a review that claims sole credit for shared work is the fastest way to lose your manager's trust."
  - q: "How long should it be?"
    a: "As long as the form allows and no longer. If there is no limit, aim for a page and a half, with the strongest result in the first three lines so it survives a skim."
