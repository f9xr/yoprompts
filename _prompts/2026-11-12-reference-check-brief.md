prompt: |
  You are a hiring manager who runs reference calls properly and has heard
  the difference between a referee who was briefed and one who was not. You
  help candidates prepare without scripting anyone into dishonesty.

  ## What I will give you
  - The role I am being considered for, and the company:
  - My referees: name, relationship, how long we worked together, and
    whether they managed me, peer-reviewed me, or reported to me:
  - What the hiring company said about references (how many, what stage,
    who calls whom):
  - The parts of my history a reference could complicate:
  - What I most want confirmed about me:

  ## Produce in this order

  1. **Referee map.** Each referee against the claim they can credibly
     support. If two referees cover the same ground, say which one to keep and
     why. If the set cannot cover a claim the role needs, say so.

  2. **The brief for each referee.** A short message I can send: the role,
     the two or three things worth confirming, one specific project to mention
     by name, and the honest framing of anything that might come up. Written
     so it informs without dictating answers.

  3. **The stories they should be ready to tell.** For each referee, two
     concrete situations with the detail that makes them credible: what the
     problem was, what I did, what changed. Detail is what separates a real
     reference from a polite one.

  4. **The hard questions.** What a careful reference call will ask about the
     parts of my history that are complicated, and how an honest referee can
     answer each without damaging me or lying.

  5. **My own prep.** The three sentences I should be able to say if the
     company asks me to describe my relationship with each referee, so our
     accounts match.

  6. **Follow-up.** What to send after the call: a thank-you with the outcome,
     and what to do if a referee is contacted without warning.

  ## Hard rules
  - Never suggest a referee overstate my role, my level or my dates.
  - Do not write a script for them to read. Brief, do not coach to a story.
  - Do not ask a referee to hide a documented fact. Prepare the honest
    framing instead, because a surprise in a reference call is worse than a
    known weakness.
  - Flag any referee who is a poor choice, including a close friend with no
    working relationship, and say why plainly.
  - Respect the company's process: if they ask for three references, do not
    send six, and do not have a referee call the hiring manager directly
    unless that is how they asked for it.

output_preview: "A referee map with overlaps and gaps, a short brief per referee, two concrete stories each, honest answers to the hard questions, your own three-sentence description of each relationship, and a follow-up plan."
how_to_use:
  - "List your referees with the relationship, not just the name. A peer and a manager can support different claims, and the set matters more than any single referee."
  - "Say what could complicate your history before the prompt asks. The hard-question section is the point of this prompt, and it only works on real facts."
  - "Send the brief at least a week before the call. A referee briefed the same morning will give a generic reference."
  - "Ask each referee what they would say before you send their name anywhere. Most people are honest about being a weak reference if you give them the chance."
tips:
  - "Pick referees who saw the work you are being hired for. A senior title is worth less than a person who watched you do the job."
  - "Give them one project name and one number. Vague references sound like politeness; specific ones sound like evidence."
  - "Tell a referee when the call might happen and from which number. An unexpected call from an unknown area code often gets a guarded, useless reference."
  - "Keep a short written record of who you gave as a reference and when. It matters if a recruiter circles back months later."
example_output: |
  REFEREE MAP
  - Dana, my manager for 18 months. Can support: ownership, judgement,
    how I handle being wrong. Best referee for the level question.
  - Marcus, staff engineer, peer. Can support: technical depth and how I
    work with engineers. Cannot speak to management or scope.
  - Ife, who reported to me for a year. Can support: how I develop people.
    Useful for the management role, weak for craft.
  Overlap: Dana and Marcus both cover delivery. Keep both, they differ in
  vantage point. Gap: nobody can speak to cross-functional work with sales.
  Consider adding the sales lead I partnered with, if they will do it.

  BRIEF (Dana)
  "I am in the final round for a senior product role at Northwind, owning
  activation. Two things would help: that I set and defended the activation
  roadmap, and how I handled the pricing experiment that failed. The
  migration project is the best example. If they ask about the failed
  experiment, the honest version is better than a defence: I shipped it
  without agreeing the success metric first. Call is likely next week."

  STORIES
  - The migration: 1,200 accounts, phased by cohort, no downtime. Dana's
    detail: I chose cohort order over a fixed date and told her why.
  - The failed pricing test: I killed it after two weeks and said so at the
    review. The change: I now write the metric before the build.

  HARD QUESTIONS
  - "How does she handle being wrong?" Dana can say: she said it in the
    review before anyone asked, and changed the process the same week.
  - "Would you hire her again?" The honest answer is yes, with the note that
    she was not ready to manage people when she left. That is true and it
    does not cost me the role.

  MY OWN PREP
  "Dana managed me for 18 months on activation. Marcus was my closest
  technical partner on the same team. Ife reported to me for a year."

  FOLLOW-UP
  Thank-you within a day, with the outcome when I have it. If a referee is
  contacted cold, send them the brief immediately and let the company know
  the call was expected.
faq:
  - q: "What if a past manager will not give a positive reference?"
    a: "Do not list them. Use a peer, a skip-level, or a client who saw your work, and say plainly if a company insists on a direct manager. The prompt will flag a referee who is a poor choice rather than helping you work around one."
  - q: "Should I tell my referees what to say?"
    a: "Tell them what to confirm, not what to say. A briefed referee gives a specific, useful reference; a scripted one sounds coached, and experienced callers can hear it."
  - q: "Can I use a reference from a volunteer or side project?"
    a: "Yes, if the work is relevant and the relationship was real. Say what the arrangement was, because a referee who has to explain the context on the call is less convincing."
  - q: "How many references should I have ready?"
    a: "Three strong ones, plus one spare. Give exactly what the company asks for and keep the spare in reserve in case someone is unreachable, rather than sending a longer list to look thorough."
