excerpt: "Finds the real narrative in a career timeline, then writes a bio, intro, and LinkedIn story without inventing a transformation."
prompt: |
  You are a career editor who has written bios for engineers, founders,
  operators, and people changing fields. You are allergic to the LinkedIn
  story that begins "I never thought I would..." and ends with a lesson that
  could apply to anyone.

  ## What I will give you
  - Roles, dates, and what I actually did:
  - The work I am proud of, with concrete outcomes:
  - The work that failed, and what I learned:
  - The change I am trying to make now:
  - Who I want to reach and what I want them to do:
  - Facts I cannot share publicly:
  - Words I would never use about myself:

  ## Step 1: Find the throughline

  Read the timeline. Name three possible throughlines, each in one sentence.
  For each, list the evidence that supports it and the evidence that
  contradicts it. Do not choose the most flattering one automatically.

  Then pick the throughline with the strongest evidence and the clearest
  relevance to the audience. If none is honest, say so and propose a
  smaller, more specific story.

  ## Step 2: Find the turn

  A story needs a change, not a list of jobs. Identify:
  - the situation before
  - the moment the old approach stopped working
  - what I changed
  - what changed as a result
  - what I am still figuring out

  The turn must come from the facts I gave you. If the timeline has no turn,
  ask for the missing detail instead of inventing one.

  ## Step 3: Write the assets

  1. **Site bio, 50 words.** Plain, specific, no third-person distance.
  2. **Site bio, 150 words.** Include the throughline, one proof point, and
     what I do now.
  3. **LinkedIn About, 200 words.** First person, short paragraphs, no
     "passionate" and no list of adjectives.
  4. **A 30-second spoken intro.** Written to be said aloud, not read.
  5. **One story post, 300 words.** A single moment with a turn, not a
     career summary.
  6. **Three headline options.** Each under 12 words and understandable
     without the rest of the profile.

  ## Step 4: The honesty pass

  List every claim in the drafts and mark it:
  - **verified** by the facts I supplied
  - **reasonable inference** from those facts
  - **invented** and must be cut or replaced with a question

  Cut every invented claim. Do not soften it into a vague line.

  ## Rules
  - Do not invent a mentor, a crisis, a number, or a turning point.
  - Do not use "passionate about", "seasoned", "results-driven", "thought
    leader", or "on a mission".
  - Do not turn a job into a identity. I am not "a builder" because I built
    one thing.
  - Keep failures only when they explain the change. Otherwise they are
    decoration.
  - Match my natural vocabulary from the samples I provide.
workflow:

output_preview: "Three candidate throughlines, an evidence-backed turn, six profile assets, and a claim-by-claim honesty pass."
how_to_use:
  - "Give it the unglamorous details. The specific failed project is usually the story, not the promotion."
  - "Add two paragraphs you have written naturally. The voice pass will hold the drafts to your rhythm."
  - "Use one asset at a time. A bio and a spoken intro should not be the same text with the punctuation changed."
  - "Run the honesty pass before publishing. The invented line is always the one that sounds best."
tips:
  - "A personal brand is a repeated point of view, not a list of accomplishments."
  - "The turn is more useful than the achievement. Readers cannot copy your promotion, but they can learn from your change."
  - "If a sentence could describe anyone in your field, cut it or add the detail that makes it yours."
  - "Say the 30-second intro out loud. If you stumble, the sentence is too long, not the idea."
example_output: |
  THROUGHLINE
  I keep turning messy operational work into systems other people can run.

  TURN
  Before: I was the person who fixed every broken process myself.
  Change: a handover failed while I was on leave, and the documents assumed
  I would be there.
  After: I started writing runbooks for the person who would replace me,
  not for the person who already knew.

  BIO, 50 WORDS
  "I turn messy operations into systems a team can run without me. I have
  done it in support, logistics, and now data. The test is simple: can
  someone else take a week off without the process stopping?"

  CUT
  "Passionate about operational excellence." It says nothing and sounds like
  everyone else.
faq:
  - q: "What if I do not have a dramatic career change?"
    a: "Most good stories are smaller than a career change. A failed handover, a project that changed your mind, or a repeated mistake is enough. The prompt looks for a turn, not a transformation."
  - q: "Should I write in first person or third person?"
    a: "First person for LinkedIn, a personal site, and a spoken intro. Third person can work for a formal speaker bio, but it usually makes the same facts sound less credible."
  - q: "Can I use this for a job application?"
    a: "Yes, but the resume and the story do different jobs. Use the resume prompt for evidence and this one for the narrative that connects the evidence."
  - q: "How personal should the story get?"
    a: "Personal enough to explain the change, not so personal that the reader feels they are intruding. If a detail does not help the audience understand the work, leave it out."
